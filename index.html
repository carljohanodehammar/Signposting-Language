Speaking / Phrasebank and practice

Show your listeners where you are.

A reader sees headings, paragraphs and the length of the page. A listener sees none of this. Signposts are the short phrases that do the work of headings in speech: they say where you are, where you are going and when you have arrived. Use this page to learn them, practise them, memorise them and build your own notes card.

With signposts
My second point is about time. The study found that ... So, to sum up ...
Without signposts
And there is also time. They found that ... And yes, that is it.

01 / Why signposts

Signposts are headings you can hear.

When listeners know where they are in a presentation, they can use their attention for the content. When they do not, they spend it on guessing: is this still the first point, is this an example, is this the end? Signposts remove the guessing. They feel repetitive when you write them down. When you say them, they sound clear.

Listen with your eyes. Read the same short talk twice.
Now without signposts.

Few and familiar

Learn a small set of signposts and use the same ones every time. Listeners do not need variety. They need to recognise the signal.

Say them slowly

A signpost is followed by a short pause. The pause tells the listener that something new is coming.

03 / Choose the signpost

The right signpost depends on what comes next.

A signpost is a promise. For example promises an example. However promises a contrast. To sum up promises the end. If the promise does not match what follows, the listener is misled. Read each short piece of a talk and choose the signpost that fits the gap.

Choose the signpost that fits the gap.
04 / Name the job

Know what each signpost promises.

When you know the job of a phrase, you can use it with confidence and replace it when you forget it. Five jobs cause most of the confusion in student presentations: moving on, giving an example, contrasting, summing up and checking understanding.

05 / Memorise

Say them until they come without thinking.

Signposts are most useful when you no longer have to look for them. When you are nervous, your memory gives you the phrases you have said aloud many times, not the ones you have read. Practise in two ways. First, recall: see the job and say a phrase aloud before you look. Second, complete: type the missing word.

Recall cards. Say a phrase aloud, then turn the card.
Complete the phrase. Type the missing word.
06 / Your notes card

Choose one signpost for each step, and learn those.

You do not need the whole phrasebank. You need one signpost for each step of your presentation, said often enough that it comes automatically. Where this leads

From a route to a conversation.

Signposts carry the structure you planned. The planning itself, a clear message, a visible purpose, enough context and a presentation adapted to the listeners, is the subject of Presenting a Text. The two pages are meant to be used together: plan there, then choose your signposts here.

The same phrases do more than guide a presentation. When the talk turns into a discussion, the phrases for adding, contrasting, giving an example and checking understanding are how you join in and build on what others say. In writing, many of them return as the linking words of a paragraph, where Grammar for Writing shows how they are punctuated.

Until then, one habit. Choose your signposts before you choose your other words, and say them aloud until they come without thinking.
